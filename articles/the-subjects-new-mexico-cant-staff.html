The subjects New Mexico can't staff

By Amy Traylor, Editor & Publisher · October 7, 2026

A teaching license says someone may teach; an endorsement says what. New Mexico tracks 23 subject endorsements, and the supply across them is wildly uneven. The state holds more than 11,800 English-language-arts endorsements and nearly 9,800 in TESOL. In computer science, it holds 138. In elementary-math specialization, 56.

The core academic subjects have deep benches. Language arts, TESOL, and social studies each clear nine thousand endorsement holders; science and mathematics each sit around sixty-four hundred. Then the floor drops out. Information-technology coordinator (324), computer science (138), elementary-math specialist (56), and fine arts (8) are not pipelines so much as trickles.

Computer science barely exists on the map

Scarcity this severe shows up at the district level as absence. Among the 37 districts large enough to report (at least 150 licensed staff), only three employ five or more computer-science-endorsed teachers: Albuquerque (28), Gallup (7), and Central Consolidated (5). The other 34 — including large systems like Las Cruces, Rio Rancho, and Santa Fe — each have fewer than five, and many have none.

A privacy note, not a footnote to skip: we withhold any count below five, so “fewer than five” can mean four or can mean zero. That is the point — at this scale, a whole subject in a whole district fits inside a number too small for us to publish without risking identifying the individual teacher.

Holding an endorsement isn't the same as teaching it

Supply is only half the story. Even in the well-stocked subjects, a large share of endorsement holders aren't in a New Mexico classroom at all — the same reservoir of licensed-but-not-employed teachers that runs through this whole dataset. Roughly three in five language-arts and math endorsement holders are employed in the state's schools; the rest are elsewhere. An endorsement on file is a ceiling on what the state could staff, not a count of what it does.

Why it matters

New Mexico, like most states, has pushed to expand computer-science and STEM instruction. The credential data says the teachers to deliver it are not there yet — not statewide, and especially not outside Albuquerque. A graduation requirement or a new course standard can be written in a season; a corps of endorsed teachers to meet it takes years to build. Where the bench is this thin, the gap between policy and classroom is measured not in percentage points but in whole districts with no one holding the endorsement.
