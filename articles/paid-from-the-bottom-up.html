Paid from the bottom up

By Amy Traylor, Editor & Publisher · October 7, 2026

Between the 2011–12 and 2018–19 school years, New Mexico raised teacher pay — but not evenly. The state lifted its lowest salaries much faster than its middle or its top. In the public salary records, the 25th-percentile teacher salary rose about 27% over those seven years, while the median rose about 9.5% and the 75th percentile about 8.7%.

2011–12 2018–19

That is the signature of a wage floor rising. New Mexico's minimum-salary tiers for teachers climbed over this period, pulling up the bottom of the scale while the experienced middle largely held in place. In plain terms, a new teacher's pay moved a lot; a veteran's moved little.

A real-terms caution: a 9.5% nominal increase in the median over seven years did not keep pace with inflation. So even as the floor rose, the typical teacher's purchasing power roughly flattened or slipped. These are nominal dollars, not inflation-adjusted.

Why it matters

Lifting the floor is a real and defensible policy — it makes the first years of teaching livable and helps recruitment. But compression has a cost at the other end: when the gap between a starting salary and a mid-career salary narrows, the financial reward for staying shrinks. In a workforce already tilting toward entry-level teachers and struggling to keep them, a flatter pay ladder is one more reason the middle of the career doesn't hold. Raising the start is necessary. On its own, it may not be enough to keep people.

Figures are aggregate salary distributions from the New Mexico Sunshine Portal (STARS “Salaries for Staff Assignments”), compiled by the Impact Lab. Nominal dollars; distributions only, no individual pay published. See our methodology. Provided as-is; the official record governs.
